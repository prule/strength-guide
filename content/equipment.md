# The Equipment Guide

You do not need a commercial gym to train seriously. The **Starting Strength** philosophy is refreshingly minimalist: the barbell is the most useful piece of strength equipment ever invented because it allows you to load a full-body movement with tiny, precise increments and to lift the exact weight your body is ready for. Everything else on this list exists only to let you use the barbell safely and effectively. Buy quality once, and a home setup will outlast every gym membership you would ever pay for.

## The Non-Negotiables

These are the four things you genuinely cannot train the program without. Everything after this section is a refinement.

### The Big Four
- **The Power Rack (or Squat Stands):** Non-negotiable for training alone. A full power rack with adjustable safety pins/straps lets you squat and press heavy without a spotter — if you miss a rep, the safeties catch the bar, not you. Get one bolted or heavy enough not to tip.
- **The Barbell:** A quality 20&nbsp;kg (45&nbsp;lb) Olympic bar with a moderate knurl and a rated tensile strength of at least 190,000&nbsp;PSI. This is the heart of the whole enterprise; do not cheap out here.
- **Weight Plates:** Enough to progress for years. Cast-iron plates are ideal for slow lifts; you want pairs down to small increments so linear progression never stalls for lack of plates.
- **The Bench:** A flat, stable, upright bench rated well above the weight you will ever press. Wobble is dangerous under a loaded bar.

## Micro-Loading

**Novice Linear Progression** lives or dies by your ability to add a *small* amount of weight every session. Standard plates jump 2.5&nbsp;kg (5&nbsp;lb) per side — often too much once the newbie phase ends, especially on the Press and Bench.

### Small Plates
- **Fractional Plates:** Pairs of 0.25&nbsp;kg / 0.5&nbsp;kg (0.5&nbsp;lb / 1&nbsp;lb) plates let you add as little as 0.5&nbsp;kg to the total bar. This single, cheap purchase can extend your linear progression by months.

## Footwear

Running shoes are the single most common beginner mistake. Their soft, compressible soles are the opposite of what you want under a heavy barbell — the cushioning squishes unpredictably and leaks the force you are trying to drive into the floor.

### What to Wear
- **Weightlifting Shoes:** A hard, incompressible sole with a raised heel (an "effective heel height" of ~0.5&ndash;0.75&nbsp;in). The solid base transmits force efficiently and the heel improves squat depth and ankle position for most lifters. The gold standard for the squat and press.
- **Flat, Hard-Soled Shoes:** Chuck Taylors, deadlift slippers, or any minimalist flat sole are a fine, cheaper option — especially good for the deadlift, where a raised heel is a slight disadvantage.
- **Bare Feet:** Better than running shoes if you have nothing else, though many gyms and platforms prohibit it.

## Supportive Gear

None of this is required to *start*. Introduce each item only when the weights get heavy enough to warrant it — usually well into your linear progression.

### The Belt
- **Lifting Belt:** A 10&nbsp;mm, single-prong, 4-inch-wide leather belt of *uniform width* all the way around (not the tapered "powerlifting silhouette"). The belt gives your abs something to brace against, increasing intra-abdominal pressure and spinal support. Learn to brace *without* it first, then add it for your heavier work sets.

### Grip and Joints
- **Chalk:** Cheap, and it fixes almost every grip problem on the deadlift by absorbing sweat. Buy a block of climbing/gym chalk before you ever consider lifting straps.
- **Lifting Straps:** Reserve these for heavy pulls (rack pulls, shrugs) *after* your grip becomes the limiting factor. Novices should build grip strength with chalk, not straps.
- **Knee Sleeves:** Neoprene sleeves provide warmth and mild compression — comfort and joint support, not a strength aid. Optional, and purely for feel.

## The Platform and Environment

### The Floor
- **Lifting Platform or Rubber Mats:** Protects both your floor and your plates when deadlifting, and gives a stable, level surface to lift on. Horse-stall mats from a farm-supply store are the classic budget solution.
- **Adequate Ceiling Height:** You must be able to stand and complete an **Overhead Press** with the bar fully locked out overhead, on your toes, without hitting the ceiling. Measure before you build in a basement or garage.
- **A Mirror — Optional and Overrated:** Watching yourself distorts your head and neck position on the squat and press. Learn to feel the correct positions and, better still, film your sets from the side to check depth and bar path.
